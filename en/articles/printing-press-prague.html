City of ink and lead: how printing came to Prague

Ondrex

While Mainz celebrated a revolution, Prague remained silent. Then came an anonymous figure who feared the scribes' guild. After him, a printer who became mayor. And finally, Melantrich - the man who swallowed the competition and gave his name to an entire street.

✦   ✦   ✦

Gutenberg completed his Bible in 1455 in Mainz. The printing press reached Pilsen thirteen years later - in 1468, when local printers with good connections to Nuremberg published the Trojan Chronicle. Prague, the capital of the kingdom, waited even longer. Why? Because there was a strong scribes' guild. And guilds defended their privileges tooth and nail.

The mystery of the anonymous printer: who printed the first Prague book?

The year 1487. A printed book appears in Prague for the first time - the Psalter. But there is no printer's name on the title page. Historians simply call him the Printer of the Prague Bible, and his identity has not been proven with certainty to this day.

Why did he hide? The most likely explanation is simple: fear. The scribes' guild was organized and influential in Prague. A person who openly operated a printing press risked at least an economic boycott, at worst a physical threat to his workshop. Anonymity was the cheapest insurance.

Pavel Severýn: the printer who became mayor

A generation later, the conditions had changed so much that printing ceased to be a dangerous adventure and became a respected craft. The story of Pavel Severýn of Kapí Hora illustrates this best.

Severýn started printing around 1520 in the Old Town. He was not a hiding anonymous figure - he was a respected burgher, a member of the city council, and eventually the mayor of the Old Town between 1534 and 1537. A printer at the head of the Prague city hall. The anonymous pioneer from 1487 could not have imagined that even in his dreams.

Severýn's workshop then passed to his son-in-law Jan Kosořský. After 1557, however, the entire dynasty vanished without a trace. They disappeared without a trace - whether they were buried by bankruptcy, plague, or just crushing competition, we do not know to this day.

Bartoloměj Netolický and the rise of Melantrich

At the same time, Bartoloměj Netolický operated in the Lesser Town - the court printer of King Ferdinand I and a man with a reliable monopoly. He was a Catholic, which pleased the monarch, and he printed everything the court needed.

One day, a young, ambitious journeyman named Jiří Melantrich came to his workshop. He was diligent, smart, and patient - exactly as long as necessary.

After 1547, when the king limited the number of printing houses in the country following the estates' uprising, Netolický found himself in a tight spot. Melantrich assessed the situation correctly. First, he became Netolický's partner. Then, in 1552, he bought the entire printing house from him - or rather took it over, as contemporary sources suggest. He moved it to the Old Town and started building an empire.

Melantrich's Bible and the house At the Two Camels

Melantrich's masterpiece was the Czech Bible, which he published in several editions and from which he made a fortune. With the proceeds, he bought a house in the Old Town - At the Two Camels - and acquired the noble title of Aventinum. The street where his workshop stood bears his name to this day: Melantrichova.

Melantrich was like today's corporation that swallows up a smaller company, moves it to a better neighborhood, and turns it into a brand. - Loosely based on contemporary chronicles and modern historiography

The curse of the scribe from the Clementinum

The scribes in Prague did not disappear quietly. Legend has it that an old scribe from the Clementinum came to Melantrich's press one day and uttered a curse: "What you print in one day will turn to dust by evening!"

The curse didn't work. Melantrich's books lasted for centuries. Meanwhile, the parchments in the monasteries began to mold. Many scribes eventually ended up working in the printing houses - as typesetters or proofreaders. They retrained, as we would say today. The craft did not disappear, it merely changed its tool.

Daniel Adam of Veleslavín: the man who made Praguers read

After Melantrich, his son-in-law Daniel Adam took over the printing house - he married Melantrich's daughter Anna and inherited the entire empire along with her. Whether it was a marriage of love or strategic calculation, the sources remain silent.

Daniel Adam, later ennobled as of Veleslavín, transformed the printing house from a profitable business into a center of Czech learning. He published dictionaries, historical writings, and translations. It is said that he was the one who made the Praguers read - even those who originally didn't care to.

Thus, in a hundred years, Prague traveled the path from an anonymous printer who was afraid to light a candle, to a publishing house that shaped Czech culture. The city of ink and lead - just like Mainz, only a generation later and with an extra dose of drama.
