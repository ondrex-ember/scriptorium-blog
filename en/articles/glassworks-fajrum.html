Glassworks Fajrum: Czech game about fire, sand and furnace operation

Ondrex

Arnsdorf, 1680. You inherited a furnace, a recipe, and a debt of fifty groschen. Buyers from Haida await behind the forest, the first batch is crackling in the furnace, and your glassblower wants a raise.

✦   ✦   ✦

In 1680, the Nový Bor region - the area between Nový Bor, Polevsko, and Arnultovice - was one of the centers of European glassmaking. The furnaces burned continuously for months. The melting pots cracked. Glassblowers drank five to eight liters of beer per shift - not for fun, but because it was otherwise impossible to endure the heat at the furnace. And the glass that came from this region traveled through Haida to Dresden, Vienna, and Venice.

Glassworks Fajrum - a browser game that attempts to capture this everyday reality as faithfully as game mechanics allow - is rooted in this world. Without fantasy. Without magic. Just fire, sand, potash, and the people who hold it all together.

Setting and Historical Context

The year 1680 was deliberately chosen as a turning point for the game. This is exactly when the transition from Renaissance forest glass - the green, opaque waldglas made from wood potash and beech ash - to Baroque crystal takes place in Bohemia. Johann Kunckel publishes his Ars Vitraria Experimentalis during these very years. Arsenic oxide enters practice as a new fining agent. And ruby glass - colloidal gold suspended in molten glass - is still an alchemical secret that very few manage to replicate.

The game map covers real locations. Arnsdorf (Arnultovice) is the starting point of the glassworks in the middle of the deep forest. Haida (Nový Bor) is the trade center where the products go. Polevsko, Lindava, Česká Lípa - every place has its role and historical character in the game world.

What You Do in the Game: The Production Chain

The game is built on a strictly historical production chain, where every step depends on the previous one. You start with wood and sand and end up - if you're successful - with a ruby goblet for the royal court.

At the beginning is extraction: wood as fuel and raw material for potash production in the charcoal kiln, quartz sand from a small quarry, limestone, and beech ash leached for potash. You mix the batch - the glassmaking raw material - from these ingredients and load it into the furnace. The furnace needs a melting pot.

The melting pot (Häfen) is the heart of the entire production and at the same time the most critical consumable part. It is made by a pot maker from refractory clay - and each one lasts only a limited number of batches before it cracks. The furnace has four slots for pots, each configurable separately: one slot can hold waldglas, the second crystal, the third cobalt blue. Good slot management is one of the key tactical decisions in the entire game.

After melting comes fining - a phase that is not optional in the game, but mandatory. Without a fining agent, bubbles and striae remain in the melt; the result is defective, the selling price drops, or the buyer returns the piece. Fining agents have different qualities: table salt (NaCl) is the most accessible but weakest. Manganese dioxide is a middle path. Arsenic oxide - a rare historical novelty material of 1680 - is the most effective and expensive. The choice of fining agent affects the clarity of the glass and thus its price.

The fined glass melt cools down to working temperature and the glassblower's turn comes. Glassblowers are independent NPC workers in the game with names, stats, and traits - they arrive during the campaign, have different levels and specializations. Elias Böhm blows crystal, Marie Wagner is a teacher, and filigree is her domain. Without a glassblower, you produce a raw shard that can be sold for a few groschen - with a glassblower, goblets, vessels, window panes, cups, or apothecary vials are created, each for a multiple of the price.

How to Win: Three Axes of Balance

Glassworks Fajrum doesn't have a single winning goal. It's a game about how far you decide to go - and whether the furnace, the forest, and the people will last. Each season is a closed story with a score. Next year you start again, but with a better understanding of what you learned last time.

The Forest. Twenty parcels of forest at the beginning of the game look like a lot. They disappear surprisingly quickly. Wood is burned by the furnace, wood feeds the kiln where potash is made, you build new buildings with wood. Whoever harvests without a plan will find out in the third season that they are left with a stump and a stalled furnace. The forest is a non-renewable resource - or a very slow one. Every chopped down part of the deep forest is a decision.

Morale. The workers need a regular wage and beer. A daily ration of one jug keeps the glassworks' mood stable and productivity normal. A double ration brings a morale bonus, but with it a ten percent chance of an accident - a cheerful glassblower breaks pieces, a tipsy melter overshoots the temperature. A triple ration is a recipe for disaster: a twenty-five percent chance of an accident, and an exploding pot at night pleases no one. Morale does not plummet steeply - it erodes slowly and quietly until one morning the furnace stops.

Melting Pots. A cheap pot saves three groschen today and swallows the whole batch tomorrow. A master pot from an experienced pot maker extends melting and improves the final glass. Every slot in the furnace shows the pot's wear - watch it and replace it before you have to.

What to Watch Out For

A cracked pot in the middle of a batch is the loudest warning the game sends - but it's not the first. It is preceded by a quiet indicator of one remaining batch, which you overlook because you are currently dealing with wood supplies. The result: a lost batch, lost raw materials, -8 morale, and a repair costing time and groschen.

Fining cannot be skipped. If you try to skimp on the fining phase with cheap salt for crystal or skip fining entirely, the game reacts with defective outputs - bubbles, striae, and stones in the finished glass. Buyers return goods or pay less. For colored glass, and especially for ruby, the fining requirements are even tougher.

Ruby glass is a chapter of its own. It is a so-called strike glass: after melting it looks pale golden - the color only develops through controlled cooling. The game simulates this mechanic with a dramatic strike modal featuring an animation. Success brings the most valuable product in the entire game, +8 morale for the whole glassworks, and an entry in the Archive. Failure returns one clear crystal back and knocks morale down. Ruby is not for beginners - and historically, it wasn't either.

Worker fatigue creeps up slowly. A melter who works too many seasons without time off makes more and more mistakes. A glassblower without rest blows less efficiently and ruins more pieces. And a glassblower who hasn't been paid on time for three seasons will pack up his tools and leave for the competition in Falkenau - taking three years of experience with him.

Archive and Achievements

Every breakthrough in the game is recorded in the 📖 Archive tab. Fourteen parchment pages cover the history and technology of glassmaking - from the composition of the batch and the chemistry of fining agents to Kunckel's ruby secret. The pages are unlocked by playing, not by reading the tutorial: with the first fined batch, the first blown piece, the first cobalt blue, the first ruby.

There are fourteen achievements in four categories. Some of them are straightforward - the first ten batches, ten workers, the first export. Some require specific conditions that the game doesn't shout out anywhere: for example, blowing a piece from four different types of glass in a single season, or paying off your debt before the end of the first winter. Whoever collects achievements must think differently than someone who just wants to survive.

Endgame: Six Paths, One Forest

After mastering basic production, the game branches out into six specialization paths, each with a different playstyle, a different production goal, and a different customer at the end of the journey.

The path of crystal clarity leads through cutting and engraving to chandeliers for the Vienna Hofburg. The path of cathedral light relies on stained glass, vitrages, and commissions for Prague or Dresden temples. The ruby secret - the hardest and most rewarding path - leads through Kunckel's recipe, gold dust, and colloidal gold all the way to an imperial gift. The enamel manufactory aims for regular export of painted vessels to Venice and London. Filigree and jewelry open the path of a royal supplier to the Habsburg nobility. And the peddler export - the most historically accurate path - relies on cheap forest glass in bulk: glass carriers from Haida (Haidaer Glasträger) carried baskets across all of Europe.

In the fourth act comes the final decision, which every player solves differently: do you sell the glassworks and leave rich? Do you hand it over to your son or daughter? Or do you write down the recipes in the Archive for the region - and leave them here for the next generation?

Why the Game Was Created

The Glassworks was created as a tribute. The main author's father is a professional glass technologist. Most of the game's numbers come directly from his notes: ten tons of wood for one hundredweight of ash for three kilograms of potash, a melting pot matures for six months before it can be used, a glassblower and a pontilist must work in pairs - a cold seam is a defective product. Kunckel's Ars Vitraria Experimentalis, research by Valentová, Sedláčková, and Rohanová - the historical sources are concrete and traceable. There is a real process behind every number in the game. And narratively, the game also loosely follows the book by Břetislav Tománek - I Apprenticed as a Glassmaker.

The region where the game takes place is worth a visit even off the screen. The Nový Bor region is still full of traces of its glassmaking past. The Glass Museum in Nový Bor is one of the best of its kind in Europe. The surrounding villages remember names like Haida, Blottendorf, Arnsdorf. And glass is still blown in local workshops today - with the same technique, the same movements, since roughly the time the game takes place.

Ignis mutat res. - medieval alchemical maxim, the game's motto

You can play it at fajrum.cz. Without installation, without registration, directly in your browser. The first batch is waiting.
