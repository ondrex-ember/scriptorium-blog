Gutenberg, Fust and Schöffer's betrayal:
an ancient tragedy in the Mainz workshop

Ondrex

It was not just a business dispute. It was a story of a penniless visionary, a loan shark with a lawyer, and a journeyman who testified against his own master. And at the end - the legend of Faust.

✦   ✦   ✦

The year 1455, Mainz. Johannes Gutenberg stands before a court. In his cellar, he has a dismantled printing press, on the table an unfinished Bible - the biggest project of his life. And right now, just before the finish line, he is about to be buried by two men he considered allies.

A loan from a loan shark: how Fust financed a revolution

Gutenberg was a genius, but he lacked the fundamental trait of a successful businessman: money. To build a workshop and start printing, he needed capital. He found it in the person of Johann Fust - a Mainz lawyer, merchant, and very patient creditor.

Fust lent Gutenberg 1,600 guilders. The entire workshop served as collateral - presses, types, paper, and the unfinished Bible. Was it an investment, or a trap set from the beginning? Historians argue about it to this day.

Helmasperger's notarial instrument: a death sentence

November 6, 1455. Notary Ulrich Helmasperger drafts a document that will forever change the history of printing. Fust sues Gutenberg. He accuses him of not investing the borrowed money into the work, but embezzling it. The court ruled in favor of the creditor.

Gutenberg lost everything - presses, types, and paper. The Bible he was just finishing passed into other hands. Helmasperger's notarial instrument is today kept in Göttingen and is one of the most important documents in the history of the printing press. It is also one of the saddest.

Peter Schöffer: journeyman, witness, heir

Who was the second man? Peter Schöffer - Gutenberg's best journeyman, originally a scribe trained in Paris. A man to whom the master entrusted the most complex work and whom he probably trusted the most.

In court, Schöffer testified against his master. And the reward came soon: Fust took him on as a partner and gave him his daughter Christina in marriage. Gutenberg's workshop thus seamlessly transitioned to a new company - Fust & Schöffer - and continued exactly where Gutenberg left off.

Schöffer was such a good engraver that he is the one credited with perfecting Gutenberg's types into the form we know from surviving incunabula. - According to contemporary sources and modern typographic analysis

The legend of Faust: printing as a pact with the devil

Johann Fust was so successful as a merchant that his speed and ability to produce identical copies of books terrified people. How can one man have so many identical books? There must be something supernatural about it.

People whispered that Fust had sold his soul to the devil. The name Fust gradually morphed into Faust - and from this seed grew one of the greatest legends of European literature, immortalized centuries later by Goethe. In the eyes of medieval people, printing was literally black magic: a thousand identical pages a day, without a single scribe.

What happened to Gutenberg?

The story doesn't have a happy ending, but neither is it tragic. Gutenberg did not give up. With the help of another patron, Conrad Humery, he started up a small printing house again. He printed several smaller works. In 1465, the Archbishop of Mainz, Adolph II, appointed him a court gentleman - which brought him an annuity, clothes, and wine for the rest of his life. He died in 1468, poor, but not forgotten.

Fust & Schöffer prospered meanwhile. They published books with elegant colophons in which they proudly stated their names. You won't find Gutenberg's name anywhere there.

Why does this story still fascinate us?

Because it repeats itself. Gutenberg was a visionary developer, Fust was a tough investor, Schöffer was a careerist who backed the right horse. It could be a story from Silicon Valley, only instead of servers there were printing presses, and instead of shares, golden guilders.
